01 Method

This is not the events table read back — it is detections re-clustered from scratch, to isolate exactly one row per fire.

Raw detections from the last 15 days were pulled straight out of detections and re-clustered with the same single-linkage algorithm the live system uses (3.5 km / 8 h). For each resulting cluster, only the earliest detection — the one build_events uses to derive the cluster's first_ts and its id — was kept. For that one row, ts (satellite acquisition time) was compared against first_seen (when the poller's INSERT OR IGNORE actually wrote it).

One outlier was excluded: a detection whose first_seen lagged its ts by 11h 26m, at the edge of the 15-day window, where truncation makes the gap unrepresentative of normal operation.

02 How the satellites move

FireWatch combines two fundamentally different kinds of orbit, and that difference is most of why their latency differs.

Geostationary · Meteosat MTG

Sits above the same point on Earth, at roughly 35,786 km, and stares at the same hemisphere continuously. That lets it scan the full disc every 10 minutes — the fastest source here — but its distance and viewing angle limit it to stronger, larger fires.

Polar, sun-synchronous · VIIRS / FIRMS

Circles north–south at roughly 834 km, sweeping a different strip of the planet on every pass. Over Zavidovići that means only a handful of passes a day, but flying low and close lets it catch fires MTG cannot see at all.

Polar, sun-synchronous · Sentinel-3

Same orbit family as VIIRS, roughly 807 km, a different satellite and sensor (SLSTR), about two passes a day between S3A and S3B. Tracked as a third source, but in this 15-day window it never won the race to be a cluster's first detection — MTG or VIIRS/FIRMS always got there first.

Verified against NASA FIRMS documentation

FIRMS' own FAQ states VIIRS gives “3–4 looks a day” at mid-latitudes (Zavidovići sits at ~44°N) and MODIS gives “at least 4 daily observations” at the equator, rising toward the poles. Combined into one feed, that supports the “4–7/day” figure in sources.py. FIRMS also states NRT data is available “within 3 hours” of observation — the same number this project already uses, and close to the 2h51m this system actually measured over the last 15 days.

What the raw database alone cannot tell you

Counting distinct acquisition timestamps per day in the database (all history, 33 active days) gives an average of 2.9 passes/day, rising to 4–8/day in the most active recent stretch. That is not a contradiction of “4–7/day”: the database only records a pass when it actually produced a detection. A day with no fire leaves no row, and even on a fire day, a physical overpass that saw no hotspot leaves no row either — so a database count is always a lower bound on how often the satellites actually fly over.

Nadir means directly beneath the satellite, at a 0° viewing angle — a polar-orbiting sensor's sharpest, most sensitive view. Off-nadir, toward the edge of the swath, the same sensor's minimum detectable FRP rises (VIIRS: ~1.8–3.6 MW at 60° view zenith), because the same footprint spreads over more ground. MTG has no nadir/off-nadir distinction in this sense — it has a single, fixed viewing angle from Zavidovići, covered in the next section.

Why MTG's pixel changes shape between the satellite and here

EUMETSAT's own FCI specification gives 2 km at nadir for the thermal channels FRP is derived from (1 km for the solar channels). MTG sits over 0°E and Zavidovići is seen at roughly a 54° viewing zenith from there, so 1 / cos θ ≈ 1.7 stretches that circular 2 km pixel into an oval of roughly 3.4 × 2.6 km. Its diagonal, ~4.3 km, is where the “~4 km pixel” shorthand used elsewhere in this codebase (as the justification for cluster_radius_km = 3.5) comes from — both figures describe the same footprint, one as two dimensions and one rounded to a single number.

Delay = system ingest time minus satellite acquisition time. For FIRMS/VIIRS this is mostly the source's own processing latency — the satellite passes, NASA processes, this system polls — not a delay this poller introduces. Meteosat is the only geostationary source in this window, and the only one under half an hour, consistent with its 10-minute cycle and ~25-minute publication latency.
